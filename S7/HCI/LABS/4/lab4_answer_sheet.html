1. Pre-Lab A: Notions (no LLMs)

Say how Nielsen's 10 heuristics, Shneiderman's 8 golden rules and Gerhardt-Powals' principles are the same and how they are different.

Similarities (points): - All three focus on minimizing user cognitive load and preventing user errors. - Shared core mechanics: interface consistency, timely feedback, easy error recovery, and prioritizing recognition over recall. - All three let evaluators find design problems quickly without testing on real users. Differences (points): - Nielsen (10 Heuristics): Broad, general-purpose UI rules of thumb emphasizing interface aesthetics, navigation freedom (undo/redo), and error prevention. - Shneiderman (8 Golden Rules): Focuses on interaction flow and user control—making users feel in charge, giving clear confirmation when tasks are finished, and providing shortcuts for frequent users. - Gerhardt-Powals (Cognitive Engineering): Focuses on reducing mental effort—combining raw data into meaningful summaries and grouping related information to avoid cognitive overload.

Give one real example from Airbnb (or another app) where a heuristic works or fails. Why?

App and screen: Instagram Stories viewer Heuristic and framework: Visibility of system status (Nielsen #1) / Offer informative feedback (Shneiderman #3) Works or fails? Why?: Works. The segmented progress bar at the top displays how many stories the user has posted, which one is currently playing, and how much time remains. Tapping advances or rewinds instantly, keeping the user fully informed of their current position and state at all times.

Link these ideas to the UCD principles from earlier labs, or to an app you use every day.

Your answer: User-Centered Design (UCD) emphasizes designing around real user behaviors and minimizing mental friction. Heuristics turn UCD principles into concrete rules that keep Instagram intuitive every day: 1. Consistency and standards (Nielsen #4): The bottom navigation bar stays in the exact same place across tabs, adhering to mobile design conventions. 2. User control and freedom (Nielsen #3): The "unsend" feature in direct messages and the ability to archive posts instead of permanently deleting them give users full control to reverse actions. 3. Recognition rather than recall (Nielsen #6): Tapping the search bar immediately shows recent searches and suggested accounts, eliminating the need to memorize exact usernames.

2. Pre-Lab B: Articles

What new heuristics (not in Part A) did you find in the articles?

Your answer (points): - Support user's emotions and feelings: Designing for positive emotional experience, motivation, and delight, not just task completion. - Ensure trust and security: Building confidence through clear privacy terms, data safety, and transparency. - Enable positive social interaction: Facilitating healthy sharing, communication, and community connection. - Support user's lifestyle and identity: Aligning design with personal identity and user self-expression. - Provide adaptability and personalization: Allowing users to customize themes, layout, and settings to fit their unique context.

Are some heuristics universal (any system) and others only for one context (mobile, AI, VR)? Give examples.

Universal: Apply to any interactive system regardless of technology. - Example: "Visibility of system status" (immediate feedback is always needed) and "Error prevention" (preventing accidental data loss). Context-dependent: Apply to specific hardware, environment, or interaction types. - Mobile: One-handed thumb reachability and visibility under changing light/sunlight. - AI Systems: Explainability (making the AI's reasoning transparent) and graceful handling of model hallucinations. - VR / AR: Ergonomic comfort and preventing motion sickness (cybersickness).

Can heuristics conflict (for example minimalist design and enough help)? How would you decide?

Example of a conflict: Minimalist design vs. Recognition rather than recall / Help & documentation. Removing labels, tooltips, and explanatory text creates a sleek, minimal interface, but leaves users confused about how features work. How you would decide: 1. Prioritize based on the user's primary goal and familiarity level. 2. Use progressive disclosure: keep the main view clean and uncluttered, but provide contextual help on demand (e.g., subtle info icons, tooltips on hover, or expandable hints) so neither heuristic is sacrificed.

D. Class reflection: how can AI make heuristic evaluation faster? Where does AI fail, and where do we still need human experts?

AI can help by: - Providing an exhaustive first-pass audit in seconds, catching omitted components, and systematically mapping issues to all 10 heuristics without human fatigue. AI fails when: - Evaluating physical ergonomics, tactile responsiveness, real device performance (lag/stutter), and nuanced local user expectations. Human experts are needed for: - Testing on physical hardware under real conditions, prioritizing fixes according to business goals, and validating user emotional trust.

Observations & Discussion Points: - Applying heuristics to mobile applications requires extra attention to physical ergonomics (one-handed thumb zone) and network variability that classic desktop heuristics do not explicitly cover. - Pairing AI tools with human evaluation creates a strong hybrid workflow: AI rapidly catches superficial visual issues, leaving human evaluators free to focus on complex user flows and cognitive workload.
