--- Slide 5 ---
What Happened?
On January 13, 2018, phones across Hawaii received the message:
“BALLISTIC MISSILE THREAT INBOUND TO HAWAII. SEEK IMMEDIATE
SHELTER. THIS IS NOT A DRILL."
It had been sent by mistake. The employee was meant to run an
internal test but selected the real alert instead. The two options had
almost identical labels and sat one after the other in the list, and a
single conﬁrmation prompt was the only safeguard. The false alarm
lasted 38 minutes and caused widespread panic.

Design Principles
These principles serve as the foundation for creating effective and
user-centered interfaces.
In which cognitive psychology and visual perception insights are used
to guide the design process.

--- Slide 18 ---
I. Gestalt Principles
Gestalt is a German word meaning form or shape. Gestalt psychology is a school
of psychology that was founded in the early 20th century by Max Wertheimer,
Kurt Koffka, and Wolfgang Köhler, and is often summed up as the idea that the
whole is different from the sum of its parts. Its starting point was with the
experiments on visual perception and on how we interpret sensory input, not
just what the eyes and brain physically do.
In HCI, we borrow its laws of perceptual organization to explain why users see
some interface elements as groups, as paths, or as foreground and background,
before they read anything.

--- Slide 19 ---
I. Gestalt Principles

--- Slide 20 ---
Proximity
Elements that are close together are perceived as related, and elements that are
far apart as playing different roles.

--- Slide 21 ---
Similarity
Objects perceived as similar are grouped together, even when they are not near
each other, i.e., the same color, shape, or size.

--- Slide 22 ---
Closure
The mind unconsciously completes incomplete objects.

--- Slide 23 ---
Closure

--- Slide 24 ---
Continuity
(continuation)
Elements aligned along a line or curve are perceived as related, and the eye is
guided from one object to another.

--- Slide 25 ---
Figure-Ground
We separate the ﬁgure (what we focus on) from the ground (the background). To
make sure that the user will be drawn to the most important aspect of the UI.

--- Slide 26 ---
Prägnanz
German for conciseness. We tend to reduce reality to its simplest form. When we
see complex shapes in a design, the eye simpliﬁes these by transforming them
into a single, uniﬁed shape (by removing detail from these shapes).

II - Cognitive Principles
These principles consider the mental processes involved in user
interaction. Gestalt explains what users see. Cognitive principles explain
what users think, expect, and remember while they use the interface.

--- Slide 33 ---
Heuristics vs. Guidelines
➔
Heuristics are not strict rules, so two evaluators can interpret them
differently, which is why heuristic evaluation uses several evaluators.
➔
Guidelines are speciﬁc and measurable, so two evaluators checking the
same screen should reach the same result, which is why guideline checks
can be done by one person or even automated with a tool.

--- Slide 37 ---
I - Nielsen's 10
Heuristic Principles
➔
Nielsen’s 10 Usability Heuristics are a set of general principles proposed by Jakob Nielsen to
guide the design and evaluation of user interfaces.
➔
They help identify usability problems and improve the effectiveness, efﬁciency, and user
satisfaction of interactive systems.
➔
Nielsen, J. (1994). Enhancing the explanatory power of usability heuristics. Proceedings of
the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI ’94), 152–158. ACM.
https://doi.org/10.1145/191666.191729
➔
Book Jakob Nielsen’s Usability Engineering (1993) Book

--- Slide 38 ---
1. Visibility of system status
➔
The system should always keep users informed about
what is going on through appropriate feedback within
reasonable time.
➔
Example: When we download an application in the
AppStore or in the PlayStore, the store interface
clearly shows through a progress bar that the app is
being downloaded and how much it is left.

--- Slide 39 ---
2. Match between system and the real world
➔
The system should speak the users' language, with
words, phrases, and concepts familiar to the user,
rather than system-oriented terms. Follow real-world
conventions, making information appear in a natural
and logical order.
➔
Example: The iOS compass app is designed to look
like a real compass. This way the users can apply
what they already know from their real-world
experience about how the object works and they
don't have to learn something new before using it.

--- Slide 40 ---
3. User control and freedom
➔
Users often choose system functions by mistake and will need a clearly
marked emergency exit to leave the unwanted state without having to go
through an extended dialogue. Support undo and redo.
➔
Example: being able to recover an email deleted by mistake is an example
of an action that allows the user to have control over what is happening.

--- Slide 41 ---
3. User control and freedom

--- Slide 42 ---
4. Consistency and standards
➔
●
●
●
●
➔
Users should not have to wonder whether different words, situations, or
actions mean the same thing. Follow platform conventions. In practice, this
means:
Use the same terminology for the same action throughout the product.
Use the same visual representation for the same function.
Keep actions in predictable locations and make them behave consistently.
Follow familiar platform conventions so users can transfer knowledge from
other applications.
Example: Using the same terminology and layout throughout a website, or
adhering to standard button styles for a particular operating system.

--- Slide 45 ---
5. Error prevention
➔
The
system
should
not
encourage user errors. On the
contrary, it should try to prevent
them in every possible way.
➔
Example:
The
Ryanair
destination selection screen
does not allow users to select a
country that cannot be ﬂown to
from the selected origin.

--- Slide 46 ---
6. Minimize memory load
(Recognition rather than recall)
➔
The system should help users recognize interface elements without the need to analyze
them in detail each time, spending more energy and time.
➔
Example: Word is actually excellent for this principle because users recognize formatting
icons such as B, I, U, alignment, bullets, and font size instead of having to remember the
command names or syntax.

--- Slide 47 ---
7. Flexibility and Efﬁciency of use
➔
This best practice indicates that the system
should provide the user with different ways to
get to the content, accelerators, and custom
actions.
➔
In addition, the system should accommodate
the way both expert and novice users browse,
without penalizing either of the two.
➔
Example: Amazon's many ﬁlters are a good
example of this heuristic. Users can ﬁnd the
product they want in many different ways
depending on their needs, their priorities, and
the way they use to browse.

--- Slide 48 ---
8. Aesthetic and minimalist design
➔
Information that is not very relevant or rarely
used should not be included in the interface.
Instead, show only the elements necessary for
the action to be carried out. This way, you will
create UIs that are easier to understand and to
use, and you will allow the users to focus on
what is really important to them.
➔
Example: YouTube keeps the video as the main
focus, with essential controls visible when
needed and less frequently used options
hidden in menus, reducing clutter and
distractions.

--- Slide 49 ---
9. Help users recognize, diagnose, and
recover from errors
➔
Error messages should be expressed in plain
language (no codes), precisely indicate the
problem, and constructively suggest a
solution.
➔
Example: Error messages must clearly indicate
the errors, their causes, and how to ﬁx them.
Speciﬁc messages like “Insert a password of
at least 8 characters” are always better than
generic messages like “invalid password”

--- Slide 50 ---
10. Help and documentation
➔
The interface should always provide help and
documentation for the user. It has to be easy
to ﬁnd, focused on the tasks and steps they
need to carry out, and not too long.
➔
Example: The AirBnB app offers different help
and contact options.

--- Slide 51 ---
II - Schneiderman's
8 Golden Rules
➔
They are a set of design principles proposed by Ben Shneiderman to guide the
development of consistent, user-friendly, and efﬁcient interfaces.
➔
Shneiderman, B. (1987). Designing the User Interface: Strategies for Effective
Human-Computer Interaction (2nd ed.). Addison-Wesley. Book

--- Slide 52 ---
1. Strive for
Consistency
➔
Use the same design patterns and terminology
throughout the interface. Maintain consistency in
layout, color schemes, fonts, and button styles.
➔
Example: Using the same icon for "search" across
different sections of a website (Diffrent Platforms).

--- Slide 53 ---
1. Strive for
Consistency

--- Slide 54 ---
2. Enable Frequent
Users to Use
Shortcuts
➔
Provide shortcuts for experienced users to speed up
their interactions. Offer keyboard shortcuts, macros, or
hidden commands for advanced tasks.
➔
Example: Allowing users to use "Ctrl+S" to save a
document.

--- Slide 55 ---
3. Offer Informative
Feedback
➔ Provide clear and immediate feedback for
every user action. Display messages
indicating success, errors, or ongoing
processes.
➔ Example:
Showing
a
conﬁrmation
message after a user submits a form.

--- Slide 56 ---
4. Design Dialog to
Yield Closure
➔ The rule aims to ensure that users
receive clear feedback after completing a
task, conﬁrming that their action was
successful and the process is complete.
➔ Offer clear beginnings, middles, and ends
for interactions.
➔ Example: A multi-step checkout process
with a clear progress indicator.

--- Slide 57 ---
5. Offer Simple
Error Handling
➔ Design the system to prevent errors
whenever possible. Provide clear and
informative
error
messages
with
constructive solutions.
➔ Example: Instead of a vague "error"
message, say "Invalid email address.
Please enter a valid email."

--- Slide 58 ---
6. Permit Easy
Reversal of
Actions
➔ Allow users to undo actions easily,
reducing anxiety and encouraging
exploration. Provide "undo" buttons or
clear ways to revert to previous states.
➔ Example: Offering an "undo" option after
deleting an email. (Ctrl + Z)

--- Slide 59 ---
7. Support Internal
Locus of Control
➔ Give users a sense of control over the
system. Avoid unexpected actions or
changes in the UI.
➔ Example: Allowing users to customize
their preferences and settings.

--- Slide 60 ---
8. Reduce
Short-Term
Memory Load
➔ Keep displays simple and concise,
minimizing the information users need to
remember. Provide clear labels and visual
cues to aid recognition.
➔ Example: Displaying frequently used
options in a toolbar instead of burying
them in menus. (MS Ofﬁce frequant used
Options; YouTube Play/Pause)

--- Slide 61 ---
III - Platform-Speciﬁc
Guidelines

--- Slide 62 ---
They are sets of recommendations provided by operating
system developers (like Apple, Google, and Microsoft) to
help designers create apps that feel at home on their
platforms. They ensure consistency and familiarity for
users: navigation, layout, typography, accessibility, color,
motion,...
1. Apple's Human Interface Guidelines (HIG) for iOS
2. Google's Material Design for Android
3. Microsoft's Fluent Design System for Windows
