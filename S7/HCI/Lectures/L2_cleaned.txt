--- Slide 25 ---
Consistency
Consistency means that similar elements, actions, terminology, and behaviors should be
presented and operate in similar ways across an interface or product.
The user should not have to relearn how the system (Software/Hardware) works when moving from
one part of it to another.

Feedback
The information returned by a system after a user's action, allowing the user
to understand whether the action occurred and what its result was. I.e., every
user action should receive appropriate interface feedback, with the amount of
feedback proportional to the importance of the action.

Affordance &
Constraints
An affordance is a possible action that an object or
interface element supports. It makes possible actions
understandable and inappropriate actions difﬁcult or
impossible.
Each object is drawn with the action it invites: an arrow
pressing down into the button, a key stroke feeding a letter
into the text ﬁeld.
The shape tells you what to do before you read a
single label.

--- Slide 43 ---
Affordance &
Constraints
Constraint:
A constraint limits the actions available to the
user, helping prevent inappropriate or erroneous
actions.
Norman identiﬁes affordances and constraints
among the fundamental concepts that help users
understand what can be done with a system.

Affordance
Failure
The visual signal (grey, the same as the past dates) tells you
"you can't pick this," but the system's actual behavior would
let you. The signiﬁer is lying about what the interface truly
affords. That's an affordance problem; the same grey styling
is reused for two situations with completely different
underlying rules (truly blocked vs. actually available), so
users can't tell them apart.
The calendar didn't fail to block anything, it failed to tell
users what could still be clicked

Mapping the User:
Beyond the Person at the Machine
Every bed on a cardiac ward is connected to a
monitor that tracks heart rate, oxygen levels,
and breathing and sounds an alarm when
something crosses a dangerous threshold. We'll
follow this one system through all three types of
users.
Scenario: a hospital
patient-monitoring and
alarm system.

--- Slide 58 ---
Mapping the User:
Beyond the Person at the Machine
Primary user: A primary user interacts with the
system directly to achieve their own goal.
Primary user: the nurse
In this scenario, the nurse is the primary user.
She sets the alarm thresholds for each patient,
watches the monitor, responds when it sounds,
and silences it once she's assessed the
situation. The system exists for her to act on,
and she's trained to operate it.
Scenario: a hospital
patient-monitoring and
alarm system.

--- Slide 59 ---
Mapping the User:
Beyond the Person at the Machine
Secondary user: A secondary user interacts with the
system occasionally, or on someone else's behalf, usually
without the training or context the primary user has.
Secondary user: the visiting family member
In this scenario, a family member sitting at the bedside
hears the alarm, ﬁnds it distressing or disruptive, and
presses the button to silence it, without understanding
what it means or whether it's safe to do so. They're
interacting with the real interface, but not to achieve the
system's actual purpose, and not with the knowledge
required to do it safely.
Scenario: a hospital
patient-monitoring and
alarm system.

--- Slide 60 ---
Mapping the User:
Beyond the Person at the Machine
Indirect user: An indirect user is affected by the system without
ever operating it themselves. The system exists because of
them, but they are its subject, not its operator.
Indirect user: the patient. Why the patient counts as indirect,
not primary
This is the detail worth spending real time on, since it's
counterintuitive. The patient is wearing the sensors, and the
alarm exists entirely for their beneﬁt, but they never conﬁgure a
threshold, silence an alert, or read a readout. Their well-being
depends completely on how well the other users operate the
system around them. This category was built to address the
gap between the person the system is for and the person who
touches the interface.
Scenario: a hospital
patient-monitoring and
alarm system.

--- Slide 61 ---
Mapping the User:
Beyond the Person at the Machine
As a conclusion: HCD/ UCD/ and ISO 9241-210
Have you actually understood all three users, not just the one typing?
The standard says design must be based on real understanding of users
and context, involve them throughout, be tested and iterated with
evidence, and bring in different disciplines to see the full picture. In our
hospital case, that means the engineer, the nurse, and even the patient's
experience all need a seat at the table, not just the person holding the
device

Case Study:
The $300 Million Button
A Checkout That Blocked
Purchases

--- Slide 63 ---
The Problem:
A major e-commerce retailer required customers to Login or Register before
Checkout. Designers assumed registration would make future purchases easier,
but the form was actually preventing customers from completing purchases.

--- Slide 64 ---
What did UCD reveal?
The testing showed that ﬁrst-time customers did not want to create an
account simply to buy something. Existing customers frequently
struggled to remember their login information, with 45% of customers
having multiple registrations and around 160,000 password requests per
day.

--- Slide 65 ---
The UX/UI Solution
The team removed Register and replaced it with Continue, with a message explaining that an
account was not required. Customers could proceed directly to checkout and create an account
later if they wished.
